Evolvent AI · May 2026 – now

Agentic data & self-improvement

Data that can’t lie, and agents that improve. A data engine whose every trajectory is verified before it ships, and a lab where frontier agents run AI research on their own, round after round.

  1. Agentic data synthesis engine · architect

    Every trajectory earns its way out

    The engine I architected works like a factory line. Author agents turn benchmarks and real engineering issues into executable tasks. A program, not a model, decides whether a solution passes. Then reviewer agents from different model families go over every trajectory looking for the ways an agent can cheat its way to a pass, and nothing ships until it has survived all of that. That line powers our recurring post-training data deliveries to ByteDance, Alibaba Qwen and Zhipu GLM.

    • Author agents
    • Programmatic verification
    • Reward-hack review

  2. Recursive self-improvement · co-lead

    Agents that run AI research

    RSIGym (code), which I co-lead, hands a frontier agent a base model and a budget and asks it to do research end to end: synthesize data, train with LoRA, evaluate in sandboxes, then do it again with what it learned. I co-designed RSI-Index, our score for how much of the remaining gap an agent actually closes. RSIBench-Data (code) casts agents as post-training data researchers. Gödel Forest (code) runs parallel agents that co-evolve by passing each other short lessons instead of repeating each other’s mistakes. The finding I keep coming back to: agents improve fast, but they don’t know when to stop. Teaching them when to stop is what I want to solve next.

    • RSIGym & RSI-Index
    • RSIBench-Data
    • Gödel Forest

RL environments & game dev

RL environments & game dev

Worlds that push back. Three environments I built at Evolvent, each one a small game that checks what actually happens, not what looks right. Top AI companies bought all three.

  1. 3D asset-repair benchmark · Blender · bought by 3 top AI companies

    Fix the mesh. Don’t delete it.

    The agent gets a production mesh that I broke on purpose: a hole in the geometry, UVs stacked on top of each other, a pivot in the wrong place, a collider that no longer matches, and often several of these at once. Its job is to repair it inside Blender until a real game engine would accept it. No model grades the result. Dozens of programmatic checks run instead, including a headless Godot import, plus a Hausdorff-distance bound against the original mesh, so the fastest fix of all, deleting the broken part, counts as a failure. Models handle one defect well. Give them two that interact and they fall apart. Retopology is the classic trap: it quietly destroys the UVs it was supposed to leave alone.

    • Stacked defects
    • Headless Godot import
    • No delete-to-pass

  2. 3D level-reconstruction RL sandbox · Godot · bought by 3 top AI companies

    Looks beatable. Isn’t.

    The agent sees a handful of screenshots of a 3D platformer, from one to six, and has to rebuild the level so a player could finish it. I generate levels path first: a reachable route comes first, then the platforms, obstacles and occlusion around it, so every level is solvable and the curriculum never runs out. Half the reward is how well the layout matches. The other half is a navmesh bot that actually tries to play the rebuild, and that is where the surprises live. Plenty of rebuilds look nearly perfect and cannot be beaten: a gap a hair too wide, a ledge a little too high. Agents also tried to cheat. One carpeted the whole floor with platforms; another put the goal next to the spawn. Those hacks are closed now, by an isolated verifier and checks the agent never sees. The whole sandbox runs on CPUs, cheap and massively parallel.

    • Path-first generator
    • Navmesh playtest
    • Hidden checks

  3. Agentic GameDev benchmark · Godot 4 · bought by top AI companies

    Ship a whole game from a spec

    A coding agent gets a gameplay spec and nothing else, and has to deliver a complete, runnable Godot 4 3D game. I score it on Mechanics, Depth, Visuals and Art. The hard part was making those scores trustworthy. An agent-written adapter binds the rubric to each project’s live runtime state, so fixed tests can check that a raycast actually hits, a collision actually fires, a resource actually settles. A VLM watches recordings of the gameplay, and its verdicts are cross-checked against that state. The rubrics were calibrated on hidden reference games. What I found: games look right but don’t play right. The screenshots are gorgeous; the hero walks straight through the wall. Mechanics are by far the weakest thing agents produce.

    • Runtime-bound rubric
    • VLM cross-checked
    • Hidden reference games
